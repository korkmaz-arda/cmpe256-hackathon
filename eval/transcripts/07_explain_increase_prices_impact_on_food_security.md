*Sub-question:* Using the UN FAO SOFI 2023 and 2024 reports, explain how increases in food prices affect food security, including impacts on hunger, food insecurity, malnutrition, and the main transmission channels and drivers.

SOFI 2023 and 2024 describe food-price increases as a core channel through which shocks reduce access to food: higher food, energy and input prices raise the cost of a healthy diet, squeeze real incomes, disrupt supply chains, and make nutritious foods less affordable. The effects show up in hunger remaining above pre-pandemic levels, elevated moderate/severe food insecurity, reduced diet quality, and risks of multiple forms of malnutrition. The reports emphasize that price shocks rarely act alone: conflict, climate extremes, economic slowdowns/downturns, inequality, unhealthy food environments and weak affordability of nutritious foods interact and compound impacts.

**Findings**
- (✅ confirmed) Food prices affect food security mainly through an affordability channel: when food prices rise faster than incomes, households’ economic access to food declines. SOFI 2023 defines affordability as the ability to buy foods locally, with diet cost assessed relative to income after other required expenses. [S5]
- (✅ confirmed) SOFI 2024 reports that food prices rose throughout 2022, increasing the global average cost of a healthy diet from 3.56 PPP dollars per person per day in 2021 to 3.96 PPP dollars in 2022; COVID-19 disruptions and the war in Ukraine contributed to higher international food and energy prices and inflationary pressure. [S17]
- (✅ confirmed) SOFI 2023 reports that the cost of a healthy diet rose globally by 4.3 percent, and that from 2020 to 2021 increases were larger in low- and middle-income countries: 6.2 percent in LMICs, 5.1 percent in UMICs, 4.7 percent in LICs, versus 2.1 percent in HICs. [S2, S4]
- (✅ confirmed) Higher food prices contributed to unaffordability of healthy diets: SOFI 2023 estimates about 3.14 billion people, or 42 percent of the world population, could not afford a healthy diet in 2021, down from 3.19 billion, or 43 percent, in 2020. [S4, S8]
- (✅ confirmed) The effect on hunger depends on the balance between income recovery and price inflation. SOFI 2023 explains that 2022 hunger trends reflected both an income effect from economic recovery and a price effect from high inflation; nevertheless hunger remained far above pre-pandemic levels globally and in all regions. [S1, S7]
- (✅ confirmed) Regional impacts differed. In Southern Africa, increased hunger in 2022 stemmed from inflation after international commodity-price increases, plus unemployment and shock vulnerability; in Western Asia, conflict, political instability, disrupted supplies and high domestic inflation made access to food harder; in Southern Asia, sustained growth, especially in agriculture, likely outweighed inflation and improved food-security conditions. [S1, S3]
- (✅ confirmed) Food-price increases can worsen malnutrition through diet-quality effects. SOFI 2024 states that food insecurity can be associated with lower consumption of all food types, a higher share of dietary energy from staples, lower consumption of nutritious foods, and/or higher consumption of energy-dense foods high in unhealthy fats, sugars and salt—pathways linked to undernutrition, micronutrient deficiencies, overweight and obesity. [S16]
- (✅ confirmed) SOFI 2024 frames price-related food insecurity as part of a broader driver system: conflict, climate variability and extremes, economic slowdowns/downturns, lack of access to and unaffordability of nutritious foods, unhealthy food environments, and high persistent inequality. These drivers are occurring more frequently, intensely and concurrently, especially in many low- and middle-income countries. [S13, S15, S11]
